Basics

How research works

You don't need a degree to contribute to science, but knowing how research is planned, checked and published makes your contribution count for more.

What is citizen science?

Citizen science is research done with the help of members of the public. Volunteers collect observations, classify images, run computations, design experiments or ask the questions themselves. The results feed real studies, datasets and published papers.

It is older than the term. Amateurs kept weather records, counted birds and discovered comets long before "citizen science" entered the Oxford English Dictionary in 2014. The internet made it possible for millions of people to join in at once.

Contributory

Scientists design the project; volunteers mainly collect or process data. Galaxy Zoo and eBird work this way.

Collaborative

Volunteers also help refine the design, analyse data or spread results. Many forum-driven discoveries fall here.

Co-created

Scientists and the public work together at every stage, from choosing the question to publishing. Common in community health and environmental monitoring.

Good practice. The European Citizen Science Association's Ten Principles of Citizen Science set out what volunteers should be able to expect: a real scientific outcome, feedback on how their data are used, acknowledgement in results, open data where possible, and respect for privacy.

The scientific method in practice

Real research loops back on itself more than textbook diagrams suggest, but the core stages hold.

  1. Observe and ask

    Notice something unexplained. "Why is this galaxy green?" is a valid start.

  2. Form a hypothesis

    Propose an explanation that makes a testable prediction.

  3. Collect data

    Follow a fixed protocol so results can be compared and repeated.

  4. Analyse

    Use statistics to separate real patterns from noise and bias.

  5. Share and check

    Publish methods and data so others can test, replicate or refute the result.

Anatomy of a research paper

Most papers follow the IMRaD structure. Knowing what each part answers lets you read efficiently.

AbstractWhat's the short version?

A 150–300 word summary. Read this first to decide whether to keep going.

IntroductionWhy does it matter?

The question, what's already known, and the gap this work fills.

MethodsWhat exactly was done?

Enough detail for someone else to repeat it. Look here for sample sizes.

ResultsWhat was found?

Data, figures and statistics, ideally without interpretation.

DiscussionWhat does it mean?

Interpretation, limitations and open questions. Often ends with future work.

Reading order that works: abstract, then figures and their captions, then the discussion, then methods if you need the details. Keep a list of unfamiliar terms and look them up afterwards.

Peer review, preprints and open access

Peer review

Before a journal publishes a paper, an editor sends it to independent experts. They check the methods, logic and claims, and the authors revise. Review catches many problems but not all, so a published paper is a strong claim, not a final truth.

Preprints

A preprint is a paper shared publicly before peer review, on servers such as arXiv (physics, maths, CS, astronomy) or bioRxiv (biology). They spread results quickly; read them with extra care.

Open access

Open-access papers are free to read. If a paper is paywalled, check the authors' websites, the preprint server, or the Unpaywall browser extension for a legal free copy.

DOIs and ORCID

A DOI is a permanent link to a paper or dataset. An ORCID iD is a free, permanent identifier for you as a researcher, useful if your name appears on papers or data credits.

Glossary

Protocol
The fixed set of steps for collecting data. Following it exactly is what lets thousands of people's data be combined.
Classification
Labelling an image, sound or record. Projects usually show each item to several volunteers and combine their answers.
Research grade
iNaturalist's label for an observation with a photo, date, location and a community-agreed identification. These records are shared with GBIF.
Volunteer computing
Donating spare processing power through software such as BOINC, which runs scientific calculations in the background.
Transit
A planet passing in front of its star, causing a small, regular dip in brightness.
Formalisation
Writing a proof in a language a computer can check, such as Lean or Coq.
Training data
Labelled examples a machine-learning model learns from. Its quality and diversity shape what the model can do.
Replication
Repeating a study to see whether the result holds. A finding that replicates is far more trustworthy.
